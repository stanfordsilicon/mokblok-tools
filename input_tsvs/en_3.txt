Welcome to “Emoji for Everyone,” a global project launched by SILICON to translate emoji into every language in the world! 

🧠 How to Translate an Emoji


Imagine you're texting a friend and type “dog” to find the 🐶 emoji. Easy, right? But what if your keyboard is in French, Italian, or Chinese? You’d type “chien,” “cane,” or “狗”—but you’d still expect the same dog face to appear.


That’s where emoji translation comes in. It's not just about translating words—it's about helping people find emojis that match their language, culture, and way of thinking.


🏷 Step One: Short Names

Each emoji has a short name. It’s a simple label like:
🐶 = “dog face”
🍕 = “slice of pizza”
🍞 = “bread”
These names aren’t supposed to be creative or funny. They’re meant to help users find emojis by typing common, expected words.

When translating a name like “dog face,” don’t rely on automatic translation. Ask: “What would someone in my language type to find this emoji?”


✍️ How to Write Good Short Names
Short names are the main search terms for emoji. They should be clear and easy to recognize. Here's how:

✅ Make Each Name Unique
Every emoji must have its own name. You can’t give the same name to two different emojis (like 🐅 and 🐆 both being “big cat”).

✂️ Keep It Short
Short names should be quick to read and speak. Use “birthday cake,” not “a cake with candles and frosting.”

👁 Describe What You See
Use names that describe what the emoji shows. 🍔 should be “hamburger,” not “fast food sandwich.”

🔁 Be Consistent
If 📫 is “mailbox,” 📬 shouldn’t be “post box.” Stick with one naming style.

🌍 Use Natural Language
Don’t just copy English. Use words people in your language actually say. If no local term exists, borrowing English is okay—if it’s common.

🛠 Don’t Worry About Perfection
Names can be improved in the future. Just make the best choice for now.


🔍 Step Two: Keywords

Short names aren’t enough. Think of 🍞 again. Some users might search “toast” or “bakery.” That’s why we also use keywords—extra words people might type to find the emoji.

Keywords help connect emojis to real-world search behavior. And every language needs its own set.
🔑 How to Choose the Right Keywords

1. Keywords Can Be Shared
Unlike short names, many emojis can share the same keyword. “Mailbox” might be a keyword for 📫, 📪, 📬, and 📭.

2. They Don’t Have to Be Short
“Raincoat” is fine. Just avoid long phrases like “jacket for use in wet weather.”

3. Choose Only What’s Needed
Use around 3–5 good keywords. Avoid:
Grammar variants: just use “walking,” not “walks” too
Repeats from other emoji forms
The emoji name itself
Long phrases or duplicates like “umbrella” and “umbrella with rain drops”

4. Use Natural, Common Words
Use what people would actually type to search for the emoji. Don’t translate English literally.

5. Avoid Harmful or Confusing Terms
Never include keywords that could offend. Avoid:
Linking religion with words like “superstition”
Adding national identities to fantasy emojis (like 👺)
Using emotional or judgmental words like “faith” or “holy”
Stick to what the emoji shows: “cross,” “mosque,” “prayer beads.”

6. It’s Okay to Be “Technically Wrong”
If someone types “plant” to find 🍄, that’s okay—even if mushrooms aren’t plants. Your goal is to help them find the emoji, not to teach biology.

📌 Summary

To translate emoji effectively:

Short Names Should Be:
Unique for each emoji
Short and easy to say
Based on what the image shows
Consistent with similar emoji
Written in your language’s natural style
Keywords Should Be:
Useful and easy to search
Around 3–5 per emoji
Simple, natural, and not repetitive
Culturally appropriate and sensitive
You’re not just translating words—you’re helping your language community connect, communicate, and be seen.

By choosing the right short names and keywords, you make emoji easier for everyone to use. 🌍💬📱
